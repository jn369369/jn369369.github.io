About

A strength-based developmental science of culture

I am a doctoral researcher in developmental psychology at the University of Maryland, Baltimore County (Culture, Child, and Adolescent Development Lab; advisor: Dr. Charissa S. L. Cheah). My work asks how culturally meaningful strengths — family relationships, racial-ethnic socialization, spirituality, sense of purpose, and community belonging — help young people recover, exercise agency, and thrive within racialized, immigrant, and multicultural contexts.

My current research centers on Asian American adolescents and families, including racial discrimination, racial-ethnic socialization, parent–adolescent communication, mental health, civic engagement, and youth agency. Long term, I aim to bridge Asian American developmental science in the United States with culturally grounded research on multicultural youth in Korea, and to translate evidence into family-, school-, faith-, and community-based prevention and intervention.

My scholarship began with questions about purpose and identity at Cornell, expanded to racial and cultural development at Penn, deepened through theological training in spirituality and meaning-making, and now centers on culturally grounded resilience and positive development among Asian and multicultural youth.

01
Research Program

Culturally grounded pathways to youth resilience and thriving

How do culturally meaningful strengths help young people recover, find purpose, exercise agency, and thrive?

❧

My research program treats culture as a developmental system — values, histories, language, family roles, spirituality, socialization, and institutions, not merely race or ethnicity. Across three connected lines, I examine risk without defining youth by risk, centering assets, recovery, adaptation, purpose, contribution, and thriving.

Developmental Contexts

Racialization, discrimination, migration, acculturative demands, multicultural stigma, family and school challenges

Culturally Meaningful Assets

Family relationships, racial-ethnic socialization, identity, spirituality, purpose, community belonging, critical consciousness

Resilience Processes

Meaning-making, coping, co-regulation, social support, hope, identity integration, agency, recovery

Developmental Outcomes

Emotional well-being, mental health, resilience, purpose, positive youth development, civic engagement, thriving

Translation

Culturally grounded family, school, faith, community, digital, and policy interventions

02
Three Research Lines

The program in depth

I
Line 1

Spirituality, Purpose, & Resilience in Youth Development

How do religiosity, spirituality, meaning-making, and sense of purpose promote resilience, emotional well-being, prosocial development, and contribution among adolescents and emerging adults?

Spirituality and purpose are frequently meaningful in the lives of youth and families, yet developmental research often measures them narrowly or treats them as peripheral. This line examines when, how, and for whom spiritual and purpose-related resources support development — while also recognizing that religious contexts can generate pressure, conflict, or obligation.

Does spirituality foster emotional well-being through sense of purpose and meaning-making?
Can purpose buffer or accelerate recovery from discrimination, family stress, or acculturative stress?
How do adolescents distinguish supportive spiritual coping from pressure, guilt, or obligation?
II
Line 2

Cultural & Family Processes in Asian American Youth Resilience and Agency

How do Asian American youth and families understand and navigate racialized experiences, and how do cultural and family processes foster resilience, agency, purpose, mental health, and civic contribution?

Building on my doctoral training and strongest publication pipeline, this line connects racialized stress with family processes and youth strengths — recognizing bidirectional influence between parents and adolescents, and moving from documenting harm toward explaining culturally grounded resilience, socialization, meaning-making, and youth action.

Which racial-ethnic socialization messages protect mental health, and under what relational conditions?
How do parents and adolescents converge, diverge, and negotiate meaning after racialized events?
How do adolescents socialize parents and influence family understanding of race, culture, and civic engagement?
III
Line 3

Culturally Grounded Mental Health & Positive Development among Multicultural Youth in Korea

What culturally specific family, school, spiritual, and community factors promote mental health, belonging, resilience, purpose, and positive development among multicultural youth in Korea?

This line builds toward research leadership in Korea — not as an application of U.S. theories to a Korean population, but by identifying culturally and institutionally specific developmental processes and building measures, partnerships, and interventions from the lived experiences of youth, families, educators, faith leaders, and service providers.

What strengths and forms of belonging do multicultural adolescents in Korea name for themselves?
How do family obligation, cultural brokering, and language gaps shape identity and well-being?
How can family, school, faith, and community programs promote resilience and purpose?
03
Methodological Signature

How I study these questions

My work combines longitudinal, dyadic, qualitative, quantitative, and mixed-method developmental research, progressively strengthened by culturally responsible emerging methods.

Longitudinal & Dyadic Designs

Multi-wave studies of Asian American families; parent–adolescent dyadic and observational research; structural equation and cross-lagged modeling.

Qualitative & Mixed Methods

Codebook development, bilingual interviewing, culturally informed interpretation, and integration of qualitative and quantitative evidence.

Community-Engaged Research

Recruitment and co-design with families, schools, faith communities, and service organizations in the U.S. and Korea.

Emerging Tools

Culturally responsible AI-augmented qualitative analysis, ecological momentary assessment (EMA), and wearable sensing for daily resilience processes.

Technology enters my research program only when it provides information that a conventional survey, interview, or observation cannot capture adequately. The developmental question remains primary; the device or algorithm remains secondary.
04
Guiding Commitments

How I approach the work

Strength-based science

Examine risk without defining youth by risk — center assets, recovery, purpose, and contribution.

Culture as a developmental system

Values, histories, language, family roles, spirituality, and institutions — not just race or ethnicity.

Youth as agents

Adolescents as interpreters, cultural brokers, family influencers, and civic actors.

Within-group specificity

Attend to ethnicity, nativity, generation, language, migration history, religion, and family structure.

Community partnership

Build questions, measurement, and translation with families, youth, schools, faith communities, and service organizations.

Responsible innovation

Use AI, EMA, and wearables only when they improve understanding — addressing privacy, bias, validity, and equity.
